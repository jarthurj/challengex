Every year USPS surveys U.S. households about the mail they send and receive. This dashboard brings those reports together, so you can find a figure, follow a trend and copy it into a report with its source.

Start with the Overview → Explore trends

What this site is

USPS has run the Household Diary Study every year since 1987; in FY2025 it became the Household Mail Survey. Each report runs to about 80 pages of charts and tables, which makes a single figure hard to find and long-term change hard to see.

This dashboard takes the tables out of those reports and puts them in one place. You can look up any year, compare years and household groups on one chart, and read plain-language summaries of what changed. Every value names the report and page it came from.

Built for

  • Analysts at the USPS Office of Inspector General preparing How Americans Use Mail
  • Postal partners tracking U.S. mail trends by product and household group
  • Anyone who wants a figure from the reports, with its source

Common tasks

  1. 1

    Find one number and where it came from

    Open the Overview or a year's page. Each value names its report and page; select it to open the PDF at that page.

  2. 2

    Compare years or household groups

    On Trends, pick a measure, then a household group. Missing years show as gaps, never as guesses.

  3. 3

    Put figures into a report

    Under a chart, use “Copy the values with sources” or download a spreadsheet. On AI summaries, “Copy with sources” does the same for text.

  4. 4

    Check a table against the original

    In Browse tables, open a table: it links to its PDF page and says if anything about it needs review.

How to read the numbers

Every value is sourced

It names the report and page it came from, and later reports' revisions are kept with their own source.

Checked against the PDF

Values are compared with the text of their source page. Anything not yet checked is marked as such.

Gaps stay gaps

When USPS didn't publish a figure for a year, the chart leaves it blank rather than filling it in.

Survey changes are marked

USPS changed how it runs the survey several times; charts show where, so a jump isn't mistaken for a trend.

Projections are estimates

They come from simple statistical models tested on past years, and are shown with their likely range, never as published figures.

AI text is labelled

Summaries are marked as AI-generated and stay “not yet reviewed” until a person signs them off.
